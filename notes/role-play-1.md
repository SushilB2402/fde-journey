# Role-Play 1: Evaluation & Discovery Analysis

## Overview
The role-play went reasonably well. You stayed polite, focused, and kept the meeting on time. However, you slipped into pitching and talking too much, and missed the best clue in the whole meeting.

---

## Performance Against the Three Core Rules

| Rule | Result | Evidence |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **1. Talk about their life, not your idea** | **Partly Met** | Your opening went straight to “build a dashboard”. Later, statements like “automating this will free up Priya’s Thursdays” and “working prototype” were pitching solutions rather than exploring operational reality. |
| **2. Ask about specifics in the past** | **Partly Met** | Questions like “Where do you pull the data from?” and “How much time does the team spend?” were solid. However, asking “What does a good view look like?”, “Email or login?”, and “What counts as a red flag?” focused on future preferences and opinions, yielding low-value data. |
| **3. Talk less, listen more** | **Not Met** | Across three turns, your total output was roughly 300 words against about 250 words from Dana. You spent too much time summarizing her answers and adding justifications before asking questions. |

---

## Critical Breakdown

### The Biggest Miss
Dana provided a strong emotional signal and specific past event:
> *"The report was late last month and the director wasn’t happy. That caused a bit of a fuss."*

You acknowledged it as “critical,” but immediately pivoted back to feature discussions. 

**Missed Follow-Up Opportunities:**
* *"Walk me through what happened with last month’s report."*
* *"What did the director say, and what did you do about it?"*
* *"How many times has it been late this year?"*
* *"What does she do with these numbers once she has them?"*

### Secondary Execution Issues
* **Supplying Answers:** You suggested specific numbers (“90% capacity and 30 minutes”). Dana agreed, but later admitted nobody has ever defined what counts as bad. Her agreement was fluff, not validated data.
* **Assuming the Problem:** Dana never explicitly stated that the work was an issue because it was manual. You labeled it as such and built your premise around it.
* **Overlooking Operational Signals:** Dana signaled that Priya is stretched, but you didn't inquire about her broader workload. Additionally, when Dana asked to check before contacting Priya, it wasn't explicitly acknowledged.
* **Vague Next Steps:** Ending on "I'll try to have a look at a mock-up" represents a polite maybe rather than a firm commitment. A true discovery next step requires a concrete commitment (e.g., agreed follow-up meeting time, direct introduction to Priya, or access to sample data).

---

## Discovery Findings & Data State

### Verified Facts (What Was Actually Learned)
* The director regularly inquires about appointments, waiting times, and clinic capacity; priorities shift based on recent meetings.
* Data originates from a combination of the primary booking system and several localized spreadsheets.
* Priya compiles the weekly report on Thursdays, taking up most of her afternoon.
* Last month’s report was delivered late, causing friction with the director.

### Unverified Assumptions (Not Confirmed)
* That manual compilation effort is the primary bottleneck or problem.
* That the director prefers email snapshots over dashboard access, or that $90\%$ capacity and $30$-minute waits are target thresholds.
* That a dashboard is the definitive or required solution.

### Critical Information Gaps (Still Unknown)
* Why the report was late last month, how frequently delays occur, and the operational impact/cost of those delays.
* What executive decisions or actions are driven by these weekly numbers.
* Who owns the underlying data sources and what technical access permissions exist.
