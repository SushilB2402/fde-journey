# The Mom Test: FDE Discovery Framework (Chapters 1–3)

## The Three Rules
1. Talk about their life instead of your idea.
2. Ask about specifics in the past instead of generics or opinions about the future.
3. Talk less and listen more.

## The Three Types of Bad Data (Chapter 2)
* **Compliments:** Polite social fibs or supportive remarks that feel encouraging but carry zero objective data.
* **Fluff:** Generic claims ("I usually"), future promises ("I would"), and hypothetical maybes ("I might").
* **Ideas (Feature Requests):** Unsolicited feature suggestions or architectural demands (e.g., *"We need a dashboard with 12 charts"*). For an FDE, clients often specify a solution before their true motivation is understood. **Rule:** Ideas and feature requests must be understood through their underlying motivations, but never blindly obeyed.

## Bad Questions vs. Better Questions (Hospital Dashboard Scenario)
* **Weak:** "Would a dashboard help you?"  
  **Better:** "Walk me through the last time you needed to know how many appointments were missed."
* **Weak:** "Is this a big problem for you?"  
  **Better:** "How often did it happen last month, and what did it cost?"
* **Weak:** "Would you use something like this?"  
  **Better:** "What do you do about it today? What have you already tried?"
* **Client Request:** "We need 12 charts."  
  **Better:** "What decision would that information change?"
* **Client Compliment:** "Great idea!"  
  **Better:** "Thanks. Before that, how are you handling it right now?"

## Before a Conversation:
1. Identify who to talk to: end users, the budget owner, and whoever owns the data.
2. Write down the three big questions about the client’s problem you most need to test.
3. Identify the fact that would show this is the wrong problem to solve.
4. Research basic facts and system architectures that do not require a conversation.
5. Prepare prompts about recent behavior, existing workarounds, data access, and consequences.

## During the Conversation:
1. Start with the person's situation, not your product or tool stack.
2. Ask about the last specific occurrence.
3. Request a step-by-step description of what happened.
4. Explore what they tried and what it cost.
5. Investigate strong emotions, data blockers, and unexpected details.
6. Redirect compliments and hypothetical claims toward concrete facts.
7. Speak less than the other person.
8. Avoid defending or pitching the software implementation.

## After the Conversation, Record:
* **Facts:** What have they actually done?
* **Frequency:** How often does the problem occur?
* **Consequences:** What happens if it remains unresolved?
* **Existing solutions:** What tools, databases, or workarounds do they use?
* **Investment:** What time or money are they already spending?
* **Motivation:** Why does solving it matter?
* **Contradictions:** Which of your initial problem assumptions were wrong?
* **New questions:** What data access or technical constraints should you investigate next?
